Le Bugle · No. 11 · 28 October 2026

Black October, a private key that was literally the number 4, and an agent that costs too much to give away

Pierre Sauvignon · 10 min read

The warnings about AI and cyber that started with Mythos earlier this year all arrived at once last week. PHP, the language under something like 70% of the web, turned out to have two remote code execution bugs that have been sitting there for fourteen years, found independently by two teams, one working with OpenAI's models and one at Google's Wiz. Finland and the Netherlands took government sites offline until they could patch. Wikipedia pulled systems down as a precaution. Cloudflare saw exploit attempts go from thousands to millions within hours of disclosure. In the same week, an Indian certificate authority, eMudhra, turned out to have issued intermediate certificates whose private keys were small integers; one of them was the number 4. Browsers blocked the lot and the company has since exited the certificate business.

I'm not a security specialist, but a few things look clear from the outside. First, this gets worse before it gets better. The pace so far has been set by software companies auditing their own code and researchers disclosing more or less responsibly (the PHP release leaked early and there's some argument about how it was handled), not by attackers using the same tools to find holes and walk through them. That will change. Second, the long tail of people running their own infrastructure, who haven't kept up with every patch, should probably move to managed services where it's someone's job to keep up. Which, I note with some discomfort, is an argument against vibe-coding your own web tooling. There will be a few spectacular breaches among those who don't move. Third, and this is the real question: does this settle into a new normal of far more attacks on far more targets by far more people, or into a higher baseline of security because AI found "all" the bugs that would otherwise have surfaced one at a time over the next decade? And we should assume the NSA and the Chinese already knew about some of these and had been quietly using them for years. Governments that got around encryption by buying zero-days may now lose that, which could push policy back toward weakening encryption directly.

The outlier is Anthropic, which used Claude to produce machine-checked proofs of memory safety for 123 open-source libraries at the assembly level, roughly 180 million lines of proof in a few weeks, and found bugs that years of fuzzing had missed. The pitch is that formal verification, which has always been too expensive for anything but avionics, could become as routine as fuzzing. I'd want to see that generalise before I believe it: a proof only covers the property you wrote down, and the hard part of verification has always been writing down the right property.

Anthropic's IPO filing is still expected any day, and bits keep leaking. The Journal's read of the draft has first-half revenue around $16bn, up from $1bn a year earlier, roughly breakeven at the operating level once you exclude a one-off accounting charge and, more relevantly, stock comp. The headline net loss of $80.7bn is mostly a $72bn revaluation of convertible notes, which tells you about the share price, not the business. What I'd want is unit economics, but the broad shape seems well established: inference is very profitable and all the cash goes into training the next model. Today's numbers tell you little about two or three years out. Pricing is shortage-based now, and nobody knows how much of the value of what gets built on top the labs will keep. There are also hints that growth slowed in recent months, which also tells you little. Meanwhile Ken Griffin says we're almost certainly in an AI bubble (I agree; good luck timing it) and Eric Schmidt reportedly owns about 1% of Anthropic from a Series A cheque in 2021, which is a lot of money however you cut it.

I don't care much about daily benchmark scores, but I do care about the structural shifts inside them. Meta and SpaceX fell off the leaderboards, rebuilt their teams, and are now at the frontier (Meta) or close (SpaceX). And now, as was always going to happen, China has a frontier model, and it's open: Moonshot says its latest Kimi beats Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on coding and agent benchmarks, which puts it a shade behind the very top and ahead of the rest. As things stand there's no fundamental barrier to entry and no winner-takes-all. That's one reason recursive self-improvement has crept back into the conversation: if one lab gets there first and far enough ahead, does that finally produce a durable lead? Beijing, meanwhile, published draft export controls covering 23 AI technologies, including model weights above certain thresholds. Existing open releases are exempt, but the optionality to close the door on future ones is now written down.

On to agent economics. Zuckerberg told the Q3 call that Muse has product-market fit: 10 million accounts set up in the US and Canada, about a quarter of them active on any given day, and more than a quarter of users buying things through it at an average of over $200. Meta will take a cut. But he also said Muse is so compute-hungry that he can't justify scaling it until the cost comes down at least tenfold, which he expects within a year (that's roughly the efficiency curve the whole industry is on), and that's why it isn't available outside North America. This is the thing that's new about generative AI as a consumer business: marginal cost breaks the playbook of the last thirty years. You can't make it free, get to scale, and work out revenue later. Hence OpenAI's Dots need a $100-a-month plan, xAI's Grok Bot needs $30, Claude Duet comes with the $20 Pro plan, and all of them have usage caps. The other half of the picture is that Meta and Alphabet keep seeing their ad businesses accelerate as AI optimises targeting and creative, and Meta in particular is hopeful about unlocking advertising from small businesses with nobody to run it.

Anthropic's entry is Claude Duet, a proactive agent opened as a US-only research preview by waitlist. Unlike Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX, there's no cute animated mascot, and the product thinking is clearer: it watches your connected apps, sends a morning brief, and only interrupts when something needs you. Passive observation is the most interesting opportunity in consumer agents, and that's what they led with.

Some of this is very predictable. Amazon blocks Muse and other outside agents, officially for security, liability and manageability, and in the long run because Amazon does not intend to become a dumb API behind Meta's customer relationship. Walmart took the other side of the trade and opened an Agent Gateway with spending limits and purchase approvals: it will take Meta's distribution over owning the customer. The reality, as Zuckerberg more or less admitted, is that hardly anyone is doing this yet and it won't scale soon. Most of it is slideware for now. Also this week, Anthropic extended its consultancy-and-PE route to market into Europe with a standalone AI consultancy backed with Hg, launching in London and Berlin by year end. And Palmer Luckey and Colossal's Ben Lamm raised $200m for Warhound, which will clone and genetically modify working dogs for the military and fit them with Anduril kit. Very Gibson. At least it isn't named after something in Tolkien.

Apple's shadow regulation again. Five years ago Apple changed the rules on app-install ad tracking, made some pious statements about privacy as Apple defines it, and in the process diverted tens of billions from Meta and the other ad platforms into its own P&L. Now Safari 27 blocks the domains that turn the email address or phone number you sign up with into a cross-site tracking ID, if the matching runs through code on the page. The question underneath is whether a private company should be making this kind of policy, top-down, with no debate, no recourse and no discussion of trade-offs. The honest counter-example is GDPR, where equally blunt decisions were imposed top-down with no debate, no recourse and no discussion of trade-offs.

The war in Ukraine is now largely a drone war. The short-range ones fly on kilometres of fibre spun out across the landscape like spider silk; the longer-range ones, and the interceptors that stop Russian attacks on cities, mostly run on Starlink. This month Starlink stopped working for anything in Ukraine moving faster than about 100 mph, and for days nobody would say why. The US government and Trump denied ordering it. Then it emerged that Musk had made the call himself, and reversed it after Trump phoned him, though Ukraine says the service only came fully back days later. Here's the question I keep turning over: what is the overlap between people who thought Anthropic was right to decide on its own what the US government could do with its models, and people who think Musk is wrong to decide on his own what Ukraine's government can do with his satellites? Why should it be either of their decision? My own view is that private individuals setting limits on what governments can do is a bad idea regardless of whether I like the individual. But whatever you think, the predictable result is faster deployment of fully autonomous systems that don't have these dependencies at all.

Ideas. Apollo's Torsten Slok keeps rounding up the macro data and finding AI everywhere except in the numbers: companies have given everyone a chatbot, run pilots, and see nothing on the bottom line yet. The trough of disillusionment is arriving just as the markets decide the capex has got out of hand. Meanwhile Apollo itself runs one of the largest AI chip credit books anywhere, including the $36bn SPV that buys TPUs and leases them to Anthropic. Dennis Crowley wrote about Foursquare's sale to Infillion ending in a zero for investors, which is also a requiem for the internet before it had billions of users and trillion-dollar platforms. What strikes me is that the problem Foursquare went after is still unsolved. Where do you go for opinions about places? Anything with a point of view can't get enough reviewers in enough cities to make the economics work, so it opens up to user reviews, scales, and converges on the mean: everything gets four stars. Then someone starts a new product with opinionated reviews, it needs to scale, and the cycle restarts. I don't think Dennis wanted to scale Foursquare. Scaling didn't scale for anyone. Bellingcat's latest benchmark has frontier models geolocating photos nearly as well as human experts, fast, but not yet reliably enough to trust unsupervised. WPP is sprinting to become a technology consultancy, with its own agent platform and "forward-deployed" creatives, channelling Palantir. And with Apple about to launch a touchscreen Mac, it's worth remembering how many times it explained that this was a terrible idea.

Data point: the FTC says Americans over 60 reported losing about $3bn to scams in 2025, up from $600m in 2020, and its estimate of actual losses runs from $13bn to $120bn, since most people never report. Like call centres, this is a skills arbitrage between poor and rich countries, and like call centres it is about to be transformed by AI, in this case for the worse. The one hopeful thought: maybe next year we'll all set up agents to watch our parents' inboxes and block anything that looks like a scam.
