OnSilent Case Study

OnSilent replaced a mobile phone's voicemail with an AI receptionist for Australian tradespeople and real-estate agents. It answered missed calls, transcribed and triaged every message, and called people back. I designed, built and ran the whole system solo, from the first commit in March 2024 until I retired it in October 2026: the iOS and Android app, two realtime voice services, an AWS serverless backend, an admin backoffice and the marketing site.

The product

A user picked a plan and got a dedicated local number, and the app set up forwarding of their unanswered calls to it. The caller heard a greeting and could leave a message or press 1 to talk to an AI assistant that knew the business: its services, its opening hours and, for real-estate agents, its current listings, refreshed nightly from the agency's website.

Seconds later the user got a push notification with a summary, the caller's name, a category, an urgency score from 1 to 10, and two suggested SMS replies. Urgent messages also went out by SMS, and every message could go to email or Zapier. During business hours, the AI caller could ring the person back on the user's behalf. Users could also run outbound campaigns, such as appointment reminders or follow-ups, from a spreadsheet or a voice command in the app.

The phone network took more work than the AI. Forwarding codes differ by carrier, and callers hang up mid-greeting, pocket dial, spam or stay silent. The app had to handle all of this without the user noticing.

Architecture

I chose serverless so a solo engineer could run the system with little upkeep and pay only for actual call volume. Short tasks ran as Lambda functions connected by queues, with data in DynamoDB and S3. Lambda cannot hold a live audio stream for the length of a call, so the two voice services ran as small containers on ECS Fargate, passing audio between Twilio and OpenAI Realtime.

The call pipeline

Voicemails and AI conversations both ended as a recording, so one pipeline processed both. Each step ran as a separate Lambda function fed by a queue. A single GPT-4.1 call produced the whole message at once: summary, caller, category, urgency and suggested replies. One notification then went to every channel the user had turned on. For missed calls, the AI caller rang back once business hours started.

How AI is used

I used the cheapest model that did each job well. Live voice ran on OpenAI Realtime, and everything after the call ran on smaller text models.

What running live voice AI on a phone line taught me:

  • Silence is the hard case. Callers pause, put the phone down or hang up without a signal. The assistant prompted the caller at 5, 8 and 10 seconds of silence and ended the call after two unanswered prompts.
  • The first seconds decide the call. I held back caller audio for 5 seconds so the greeting played cleanly, and a short typing sound filled the pause while the model responded.
  • Cost tracking has to be per call. Prompts ran to about 15,000 tokens per turn. Prompt caching covered about 76% of input tokens, and every call record stored its own cost.

Making it reliable

A receptionist that loses messages is worse than none, so most of the work after launch went into the cases where calls failed. Each fix below came from a real production problem.

ProblemFix
After hanging up, the AI kept prompting a dead line and spent tokens every 20 secondsA hang-up now stopped the silence prompts, and any audio connection that never identified itself was closed after 5 seconds
Transcription sometimes finished before the message record existedThe save was retried, so the message was stored once the record existed
A recording could stall between conversion, transcription and triageA job every 10 minutes picked up any recording stuck for 5 to 15 minutes and sent it through again
Pocket dials and instant hang-ups filled the inbox with empty messagesRecordings under 2 seconds and AI calls under 14 seconds were logged as missed calls instead of messages
Spam callers reached the AI and cost moneyA spam score from Twilio sent high-risk callers straight to voicemail
A scheduler running every minute could pick up the same outbound call twiceEach call was claimed by one worker only, the queue dropped duplicates, and the status was checked again before dialling

The approach was the same each time. Every step handed off through a queue, every hand-off was retried or picked up by a scheduled job, and nothing depended on a single notification from Twilio arriving.

Monetization and growth

Revenue. OnSilent sold monthly or yearly subscriptions through Apple in-app purchase, priced in Australian dollars, with a free trial. Each plan included AI minutes, billed per second: half rate for voicemail and outbound calls, 5% for a missed call. Apple's subscription notifications moved each account through 13 states, from trial through grace period to archive. Archiving released the phone number and deleted the login.

Acquisition. The website let visitors record a message in the browser and see it transcribed and triaged on the spot. For real-estate agents, OnSilent sent appraisal leads into the agency's CRM (LeadPlus / Reapit) and read their Google or Microsoft calendars and contacts.

Outcomes

Working alone for two and a half years, I took OnSilent from a Twilio voicemail script to a production AI receptionist. It handled 9,457 calls and placed 3,929 AI calls before I retired it on 1 October 2026.

  • Shipped: a native iOS and Android app, live AI voice for inbound calls, outbound calls and in-app use, a message pipeline from recording to push notification in seconds, Apple subscription billing, CRM and calendar integrations, an admin backoffice and a marketing site with a live demo.
  • Reliable: every step handed off through a queue with a scheduled check behind it, so a missed notification or a slow model delayed a message but did not lose it.
  • Retired cleanly: I told customers in advance. Before deleting anything, I archived every table, all 7,171 recordings, both user directories and the code of all 83 functions (1.4 GB), with a written guide to restart it. I switched services off first and deleted them only after checking costs and what was still running.

What I learned:

  1. The phone network is most of the work. Carrier forwarding, short calls, spam and silence took more engineering than the models did.
  2. Put a queue and a scheduled check behind every step that depends on another service. Never assume a notification will arrive.
  3. Record cost, duration and outcome on every call. That data let me tune prompts, silence timings and model choices against real traffic.
