OnReplay Case Study

OnReplay turns a handful of ordinary photos into a themed, AI-animated short film, sold to consumers on a credit model in eight languages. I designed, built and operate the whole system solo: the web app, the marketing site, the admin backoffice and an AWS serverless backend that orchestrates several generative AI providers per render.

The product

A user picks a themed "world" (a wedding, a family Christmas, a superhero comic, a pet tribute), uploads a few photos, and minutes later receives a short film starring their own faces, animated and scored. Each render costs credits, bought as packs or a subscription.

Calling an AI model is the easy part. Most of my work went into making AI services, which are slow, charge per call and sometimes fail, reliable enough for paying customers. When a step fails, the system recovers on its own, without anyone stepping in.

Architecture

I chose a fully serverless setup on one AWS account so that one person could run it, with no servers to manage. Each API request runs as a Lambda function, and data lives in DynamoDB and S3. Rendering a film is the one long-running job. A Step Functions workflow coordinates it and sends the final video assembly to low-cost Spot compute.

The AI rendering pipeline

I ordered the steps so that cheap failures show up before expensive ones: every photo is restyled before any video job starts. Each clip is saved per photo and scene, so a re-run only pays for the clips that failed. Key limits, such as the 60-minute wait, are covered by tests so they cannot change by accident.

How AI is used

Every film uses at least three AI models. I assumed any of them could be unavailable at any time, so each step has a fallback and a spending limit.

StepModelsFallback
Restyle each photo into the worldGeminiGrok, then OpenAI, with a softened prompt in between
Write the motion prompt for each clipGPT-4oA hand-written default
Animate each image into a clipKling, with Veo, Sora and HeyGen for A/B testsGrok
Join the clips, add music and a watermarkffmpeg, no AIRegular on-demand compute if cheaper Spot capacity is unavailable

What running this in production taught me:

  • Retrying the same provider rarely helps. If a provider's content filter rejects an image once, it will reject it again. The system switches provider or softens the prompt instead.
  • Fallbacks can quietly double costs. Tracking cost per film caught one fallback doing this within a day. Every fallback now has a cost check.
  • Provider errors are often misleading. "Rate limited" sometimes meant "out of prepaid credit". The system sorts errors by type to decide whether to retry, switch provider or alert me.

One pipeline, 30 products

A themed world is made of settings and prompts only, so I can launch a new one without changing the pipeline code. In ten of the worlds, users also choose their scenes from a catalogue of 105.

In practice, more bugs came from incomplete settings than from the AI models. Automated checks now block a release if any world is missing a piece, including the scenes users can pick.

Making it reliable

Most of the work after launch went into reliability. Each fix below came from a real production incident.

ProblemFix
Large albums lost scenes when the video provider hit its concurrency limitA shared counter of open slots, so extra jobs wait in a queue instead of being dropped
Slow renders were reported as failuresA longer wait window and up to two automatic re-runs that reuse finished clips, never charging twice
The provider ran out of prepaid credit and the error looked like a rate limitErrors are now sorted by type, and a monitor alerts me before credit runs out
A fallback path doubled the cost per photoI removed that fallback, and cost is now tracked per film
Cheap Spot capacity ran out and video assembly stalledJobs now move to regular on-demand compute automatically, and a monitor alerts on a stuck queue

For each incident, I made the failure visible and capped what it could cost. Then I added a test so it would not come back.

Monetization and growth

Revenue. Users buy credit packs or a subscription through Stripe, in three currencies, with self-serve cancellation. New accounts get one free film. Prices are defined in one place, and automated checks keep the web app, backend and backoffice in sync.

Acquisition. The marketing site has about 3,200 pages in eight languages, built from the same 30 worlds, plus a blog. Eight free single-photo tools lead people into the paid app, and I track every step of that funnel in PostHog.

Outcomes

In ten months, working alone, I shipped and now operate a paid consumer AI product that has delivered 3,121 films to 2,065 creators.

  • Shipped: 30 themed worlds, a Stripe credit and subscription business, a marketing site in eight languages, and an admin backoffice with revenue and production dashboards.
  • Reliable: the system recovers on its own from provider outages, content rejections and capacity shortages. Customers are never charged twice for a failed film.
  • Lean: fixed infrastructure cost stays in the tens of dollars a month. The variable cost is AI usage, which is tracked per film.

What I learned:

  1. Treat every AI call as an unreliable, paid outside service. Never let a retry or fallback spend money you did not budget for.
  2. Keep prompts and product settings in data, and test that they are complete.
  3. Build admin and debugging tools early. They paid off the first time a customer wrote in.
